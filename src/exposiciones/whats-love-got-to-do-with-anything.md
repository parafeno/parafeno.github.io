Walter Benjamin noted that in uncertain times, the masses develop an urgent need for self-expression, and that power, rather than addressing the material conditions driving that anxiety, simply offers them an outlet, a form, somewhere to put it. Today, that somewhere is the self.

Much like the work of art, we have shed our ritualistic value, our sense of belonging to something larger than ourselves, and replaced it with exhibition value, with endless circulation. The self has become a project, a brand, a never-ending act of self-production. As Catherine Liu argues, identity formation is a mode of industrial production.

Parafeno traces this global supply chain of identity: trauma repackaged as a token of belonging, the self as ongoing narrative. Within this fragmented landscape, he examines his growing fascination with the digital portrait. For Deleuze and Guattari, the face operates as an overcoding machine, a stable, readable sign that reduces the singularity of a person to something as schematic as Oedipus.

But the machine never quite seals shut. There is something uncanny about the digital face, something that keeps slipping through: a line of flight, a becoming that refuses to be coded. And it is precisely that incompleteness that drives the endless proliferation of labels, trauma, brands, surfaces of projection. The market of subjectivities feeds on the wound it never allows to heal.

If the self was constructed, and history suggests it was, somewhere around the seventeenth century, as God receded and the subject stepped in to fill the void, then it can also be taken apart. Or at least, shifted.

It is in that impossibility of closure that Parafeno places the question of love: perhaps the only force that has never needed to close in order to exist. Something that comes before identity, or after it…
